Spotting a risky clause is easy. Knowing what your team will accept is the work.

From your playbook to a finished redline.

  1. Teach it your positions

    Add the playbooks and clause libraries you already use, in Word or Excel. Rivell reads each entry into a position, and you approve every one before it is used.

  2. Drop in a contract

    Rivell reads the contract, works out which side you are on, and checks every clause against each position that applies, even when the wording differs.

  3. See the gap, make the call

    Each finding shows the clause, your position and the gap, with wording your team already approved. Accept it, edit it, keep the clause, or escalate it.

  4. Send back a clean redline

    Rivell writes your accepted changes into the original Word file as tracked changes, then reads the file back to check each one.

Built to earn a lawyer's trust.

Your playbook outranks old deals

A similar clause from a past deal never overrides your playbook. Rivell weighs each source by its authority before it looks at how similar it is.

Findings cite their sources

Each finding shows the clause, the position it was judged against, and the playbook and version that position came from.

Close calls come to you

A close call gets a second opinion. If the two opinions disagree, the clause is marked for your look instead of guessed.

No position is skipped in silence

Each position in your playbook is marked as checked, not found in this contract, or not checked, with the reason.

Most of the judgment comes from a System One model.

Jev, a System One decision model, judges each clause and says how sure it is. Close calls get a second opinion from a language model, and disagreements come to you. Plain code sets severity and checks the Word file.

Open, and yours to run

Rivell is open source. You run it on your own infrastructure, so contracts and knowledge stay in your own database. Today it reviews Word contracts in English, for one user on one machine. Read the code, or run it yourself.
