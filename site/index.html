Spotting a risky clause is the easy part. Knowing what your team will accept, and saying it in your own words, is the work.

Every legal team has positions: what it prefers, where it can give, where it walks away, and who must approve an exception. These positions live in playbooks, clause libraries and the memory of senior counsel. Rivell applies them to every clause of every contract.

From your playbook to a finished redline.

  1. Teach it your positions.

    Add the playbooks and clause libraries your team already uses, in Word or Excel. Rivell reads each entry into a clear position: what you prefer, your fallbacks, your walk-away, and who approves an exception. You check every entry against its source before Rivell uses it.

  2. Drop in a contract.

    Rivell reads the document's structure, works out which side you are on, and checks every clause against each position that applies. It also searches your knowledge for positions a clause touches in different words.

  3. See the gap and make the call.

    Each finding sets what the contract says beside what your playbook says, names the gap, and offers wording your team has already approved. Accept it, edit it, keep the clause as it is, or escalate it to the person who must sign off.

  4. Send back a clean redline.

    When every finding is decided, Rivell writes your accepted changes into the original Word file as tracked changes. Then it reads the file back to confirm that each change landed exactly as you approved it.

Change the standard, and the review follows.

Rivell has no opinion of its own about a good contract. It applies yours. Set a payment position and watch the same three contracts change their answer.

Built to earn a lawyer's trust.

Your rules outrank everything else.

A similar clause from an old deal never overrides your playbook. Rivell weighs every source by its authority before it looks at how similar it is.

Every finding shows its work.

Each finding cites the clause, the position it was judged against, and the playbook and version that position came from.

It knows when it is unsure.

A close call gets a second opinion. If the two opinions disagree, the clause comes to you, marked for a closer look.

Nothing passes in silence.

Every position in your playbook is accounted for: checked, not found in this contract, or not checked, with the reason.

Most of the judgment comes from a System-1 model.

Jev, a System-1 decision model, answers narrow questions about each clause and says how sure it is. A sure answer becomes a finding. A close call goes to a language model for a second look, and a disagreement comes to you. Hybrid search finds positions a clause states in other words, and plain code sets severity and checks the Word file.
